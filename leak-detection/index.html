If This Sounds Familiar

Water Bill Up, No Visible Leak Anywhere?

Most homeowners who call us haven't seen an actual leak. What they've noticed is a water bill that jumped for no clear reason, a meter that keeps ticking even with every fixture off, or a musty smell in one specific area of the house. That's exactly the situation leak detection is built for: finding a leak that's real but genuinely hidden, whether it's under a slab, inside a wall, or running along an underground line between the meter and the house.

New Jersey's climate adds a wrinkle worth knowing about: a leak that starts small in warmer months can worsen dramatically once a hard freeze hits a compromised section of pipe, so a leak you've been meaning to check out in October is worth addressing before winter rather than after.

According to the EPA's WaterSense program, household leaks nationwide waste over a trillion gallons of water annually, and a single running toilet or hidden supply-line leak can waste hundreds of gallons a day without ever producing a visible puddle. That's part of why a jump in your bill is often the first real signal, well before anything is visible.

How It Works

How We Find a Leak Without Digging Blind

Acoustic Listening

Specialized equipment picks up the specific sound signature of pressurized water escaping a pipe, even through concrete or drywall.

Thermal Imaging

A temperature difference from escaping water often shows up on a thermal camera before it's visible any other way.

Pressure Testing

Isolating sections of the system helps confirm which line is actually losing pressure.

Targeted Access

Once the leak is pinpointed to a small area, we open only what's necessary, not a wide guess.

Why Precision Matters

Why We Don't Guess and Open Walls Blindly

Opening a wall or cutting into a slab without confirming the exact leak location first is how a small plumbing problem turns into an expensive drywall and flooring project. Precise detection narrows the search to a specific, small area before anything gets opened, which keeps the repair itself focused and the cost predictable rather than open-ended.

Check Your Meter

How to Tell a Hidden Leak From a High Bill

A jump on the water bill is the most common reason people reach out to us. Sometimes a leak is the cause. Other times the billing period ran long, the lawn got more water, or guests stayed for a month. A short meter test usually shows which one you are dealing with.

Start with every faucet, appliance, and ice maker turned off. Then look at the dial on your water meter. Most meters have a small triangle or wheel that moves whenever any water passes through.

  • ✓
    Turn everything off and make sure nobody flushes a toilet or starts a laundry load.
  • ✓
    Read the meter and write the number down, or take a photo of the dial.
  • ✓
    Wait twenty minutes without using any water, then read it again.
  • ✓
    Compare the two readings. If the number moved or the small wheel keeps turning, water is escaping somewhere past the meter.
  • ✓
    Close the main shutoff inside the house and check again. A meter that stops points to a leak in the walls or floors. A meter that keeps moving points to the line outside.

Toilets cause many false alarms, because a worn flapper can leak for days without any sound. Tint the tank water with a little dye, wait fifteen minutes, and color will show in the bowl if the flapper is failing. That fix belongs on our toilet repair page and often ends the bill problem on its own.

Outside the Walls

Leaks on the Line Between the Street and Your House

The pipe that carries water from the street into your home spends most of its length underground. When it leaks, there is often no puddle to see. People notice a patch of lawn that stays soft or looks greener than the rest. Others find a damp spot near the meter box, or a driveway edge that stays wet in dry weather. Inside the house, everything can seem normal.

That is why the meter test matters so much for this kind of leak. If the meter keeps moving after the main shutoff is closed, the loss is outside the house. Property owners are generally responsible for the pipe from the meter to the house, but your water utility can tell you exactly where its responsibility ends.

Our approach starts with a pressure test that isolates the line from the house. That confirms water is really leaving the pipe. Acoustic listening along the route then narrows the location, and thermal imaging helps where the line runs close to the surface. Working this way keeps any excavation small and planned.

Once the break is found, the choice is a spot repair or a longer replacement. Pipe material, age, and the number of earlier breaks decide which one fits. If you believe the line is old galvanized or lead pipe, tell dispatch when you call so the visit can be planned around it. Brand new water lines are handled under plumbing installation.

Second Opinions

When Another Plumber Has Already Looked and Found Nothing

Many callers have already paid for a visit that ended without an answer. Often the search started with guesses about where the leak must be. A better start is a test that shows whether water is leaving the system at all, then which section it leaves from. Each test rules out part of the property, so the search gets smaller instead of wider.

It helps to know what was already tried. These details save time on the first visit:

  • ✓
    Where the meter sits and whether it is in a basement, a curb box, or a meter pit.
  • ✓
    What the last plumber checked, such as toilets, outdoor spigots, or the water heater.
  • ✓
    When the bill changed and roughly by how much.
  • ✓
    Any wet spots, sounds, or smells, even faint ones.
  • ✓
    The age of the house and the pipe material, if you know it.

Owners of two to four unit buildings can share the same details. In a building with several units, tracing starts at the shared supply and isolates each unit in turn. Larger commercial properties are handled through our commercial plumbing service.
