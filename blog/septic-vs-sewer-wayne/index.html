Septic or Sewer? How Wayne Homeowners Can Tell Which They Have

By the Wayne Pro Plumbing team

Most of Wayne is connected to municipal sewer service, but not every property is, particularly some homes on larger or more rural lots away from the township's denser neighborhoods. If you've never had to think about which one your home has because everything has simply always worked, here's how to actually find out, and why it matters.

The Quickest Way to Check

Your water bill is the fastest place to look. If you're on municipal sewer, your water utility bill typically includes a separate sewer usage charge, since most municipalities bill for sewer service based on metered water usage. If you don't see a sewer charge anywhere on your bill, that's a strong indicator you're on a private septic system instead.

Beyond the bill, a visual property check can confirm it: a septic system has a tank buried somewhere in the yard, typically with visible access lids (sometimes covered by grass or landscaping) and a drain field area nearby. If you've never located these features and don't have documentation from when you bought the home, a plumber can help locate them.

Why This Distinction Actually Matters

The two systems have essentially nothing in common in terms of maintenance needs. A home on municipal sewer has no tank to maintain; wastewater simply leaves the property through the sewer line to the municipal system, and your only real point of failure is the lateral line itself, which can develop the kind of root intrusion or age-related damage covered elsewhere on this site.

A septic system is a completely different responsibility. According to standard septic system guidelines, the tank needs periodic pumping, typically every 3 to 5 years depending on household size and usage, and the drain field needs to be protected from heavy loads, tree roots, and anything that could compact the soil or block drainage. Neglecting this maintenance doesn't just risk inconvenience; a fully failed septic system and drain field can be one of the more expensive property repairs a homeowner faces.

If You're Not Sure and You're Buying or Have Just Bought a Home

This is exactly the kind of detail that's easy to overlook during a home purchase, especially if you're moving from an area where every home is on municipal sewer and the concept of septic maintenance never came up. If you've recently moved into a Wayne home and genuinely don't know which system you have, this is worth confirming in your first few months, before you're facing an emergency that forces the answer on you.

Signs a Septic System Needs Attention

  • ✓
    Slow drains throughout the house, not just one fixture, often the first noticeable sign.
  • ✓
    Sewage odor near the tank or drain field, especially noticeable after rain.
  • ✓
    Unusually lush, green grass in a specific patch over the tank or field area.
  • ✓
    Standing water or sogginess in the drain field area with no other clear cause.

What This Means for Property Maintenance Going Forward

Once you know which system you have, the ongoing maintenance plan is straightforward. Municipal sewer homes should be alert to the age and material of their own sewer lateral, particularly in older parts of Wayne with clay or cast-iron pipe. Septic homes should track their pumping schedule and avoid the common mistakes that shorten a system's life, like flushing items that don't break down or running multiple heavy water loads (several loads of laundry back to back) that overload the drain field's capacity all at once.

Can a Property Switch From Septic to Sewer?

In some cases, yes, if municipal sewer lines have been extended to reach a previously septic-only area. This typically involves a connection fee to the township or utility, plus the cost of running a new line from the house to the street connection point, and often decommissioning the old septic tank properly rather than simply abandoning it in place. If municipal sewer has recently become available near your property and you're currently on septic, it's worth asking your township about the connection process and costs, since it can sometimes make long-term financial sense compared to ongoing septic maintenance, especially for an aging system.

What About a New Construction or Addition?

If you're adding a bathroom, finishing a basement, or otherwise adding fixtures that increase your home's wastewater output, knowing which system you're on matters even more. A septic system has a rated capacity based on the number of bedrooms and expected occupancy, and adding fixtures without accounting for that capacity can overload a system that was working fine before. A municipal sewer connection generally has more headroom for this kind of change, though larger additions may still require permit review to confirm the connection can handle the added load.

A Note on Well Water

Some of the same rural or larger-lot Wayne properties that rely on septic systems also draw their water from a private well rather than the municipal water supply, though these two things aren't automatically linked; it's entirely possible to have municipal water and a private septic system, or a private well paired with municipal sewer. If you're unsure about either your water source or your wastewater system, both are worth confirming together, since a property with both private well and private septic has a genuinely different overall maintenance profile than one with just one or the other, and different insurance considerations can apply as well.

Township permitting for either scenario typically requires confirming your current system type as part of the application, which is one more reason it's worth knowing definitively rather than assuming, especially before you're mid-renovation and need the answer to move a permit forward.
