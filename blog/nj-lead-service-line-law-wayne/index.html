NJ's 2031 Lead & Galvanized Pipe Law: What It Means for Wayne Homeowners

By the Wayne Pro Plumbing team

If you own an older home in Wayne, you may have already received, or will eventually receive, a notice from your water utility about the material of your home's water service line. This isn't a scam or a sales pitch. It's part of a real New Jersey state law that's reshaping how water utilities and homeowners across the state think about aging pipe infrastructure, and it's worth understanding what it actually requires, what it doesn't, and what it means if your Wayne home falls into the affected category.

The Law, In Plain Terms

In July 2021, New Jersey enacted the Lead Service Line Replacement Law (P.L.2021, Chapter 183). The law requires every community water system in the state to identify the material of both the utility-owned and the customer-owned portions of every water service line in its territory, notify property owners whose lines are confirmed or suspected to contain lead, and replace all lead service lines statewide by 2031.

A detail that surprises a lot of homeowners: the law's definition of "lead service line" includes galvanized steel pipe, not just pipe made of lead itself. That's because galvanized coatings were historically applied using zinc that often contained trace lead, and because a galvanized line installed near a lead line can absorb and later release lead particles even after the original lead line is gone. In practice, this means a home with an old galvanized service line, a very common material in homes built before the 1960s and 70s, falls under the same replacement mandate as a home with an actual lead pipe.

Why This Matters More in Older Parts of Wayne

Wayne's housing stock includes a real mix of construction eras, and the older sections, particularly homes built before 1980 near the established neighborhoods around Packanack Lake and Preakness, are exactly the kind of housing where galvanized steel service lines and interior supply piping were standard practice at the time of construction. If your home falls into that older bracket and you've never had the service line material confirmed, this law is the reason your water utility may reach out, and it's a reasonable prompt to find out proactively rather than wait for a notice.

It's worth being clear about what a lead or galvanized service line notice does and doesn't mean. New Jersey water systems are required to treat water to reduce pipe corrosion, and receiving a notice does not mean your water is currently unsafe to drink for most uses. It does mean the specific service line material has been flagged for the mandated replacement timeline, and it's worth taking seriously rather than ignoring.

Who Pays, and How the Replacement Actually Works

Service line ownership is typically split: the utility usually owns the portion from the water main to the curb stop near the street, and the property owner typically owns the portion from the curb stop to the house. Under the 2021 law and the utility programs built around it, water systems across New Jersey are generally responsible for coordinating and often covering the cost of replacing the full line, including the homeowner-owned portion, though the specifics of cost-sharing vary by utility. If you receive a notice, the right first step is contacting your water utility directly to understand your specific situation and their replacement process and timeline, since these details differ between providers.

What This Means If You're Planning Other Plumbing Work

If you're already planning a bathroom remodel, a water heater replacement, or any project that involves opening up walls or accessing your home's plumbing, it's a reasonable time to also confirm your service line material and interior supply pipe condition, especially if your home was built before 1980. Coordinating a known galvanized-pipe issue with other planned work can sometimes be more efficient than addressing it as a separate, later project.

We regularly encounter galvanized supply lines during other repair and installation work in Wayne's older homes. If we identify a section of galvanized pipe while we're already working on a related issue, we'll flag it honestly, whether it's a concern related to this law's service-line-specific requirements or simply a practical note about the pipe's remaining useful life. Interior galvanized supply piping (as opposed to the underground service line) isn't directly covered by the 2021 law's utility replacement mandate, but it shares the same aging-material concerns and is worth discussing as part of any broader plumbing work.

Practical Next Steps

If your Wayne home was built before 1980 and you're not sure what your service line is made of, contact your water utility to ask about their lead service line inventory program; most utilities serving Wayne have an online tool or customer service line for this. If you've received an actual notice, follow up with the utility about their specific replacement timeline and process for your address. And if you're dealing with a related plumbing issue, like a burst pipe, reduced water pressure, or discolored water, in a home with older piping, that's worth having assessed as its own repair need, separate from the utility-side service line question.

One more thing worth knowing: the law's 2031 deadline is a statewide requirement, not a suggestion utilities can quietly ignore, and many New Jersey water systems are actively phasing in replacements years ahead of that final date to spread out the workload. If your utility hasn't reached out yet, that doesn't mean your line has been overlooked; it likely means your replacement is scheduled for a later phase of a multi-year rollout. Reaching out proactively rather than waiting for a notice can sometimes move your address up the list, particularly if you have specific health concerns, such as a pregnancy or young children in the household, that make earlier confirmation and replacement more of a priority for your family.

If You've Received a Notice

What to Do While You Wait for Replacement

  • ✓
    Know it's still safe for most uses. NJDEP guidance confirms tap water remains safe for handwashing, cleaning, and watering non-edible plants while a lead or unknown line is pending confirmation or replacement.
  • ✓
    Run the tap before drinking or cooking if water has sat in the pipes for several hours, which helps flush out water that's had the most contact with an older line.
  • ✓
    Contact your water utility directly rather than assuming; they can confirm your specific service line status and where you fall in their replacement schedule.
  • ✓
    Ask about filter programs. Under EPA's Lead and Copper Rule revisions, utilities must provide pitcher filters or point-of-use devices following a lead service line replacement, and some offer them proactively to affected households.
