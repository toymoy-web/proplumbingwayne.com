Older Low-Flow Models

Why Some Older Toilets Clog Constantly

Toilets made in the early years of federal low-flow mandates, roughly the late 1990s through early 2000s, often used the same bowl and trapway design as older, higher-volume models but with less water to actually move waste through them. The result is a toilet that clogs more than it should, no matter how carefully it's used. Modern low-flow designs solved this with better bowl geometry, which is part of why a straight swap to a newer model sometimes fixes a chronic clogging problem that repair alone never will.

If your toilet dates to that specific window and clogs more than any other fixture in the house, that's a real, checkable pattern worth mentioning when you call, since it changes whether we lead with a repair attempt or a straightforward replacement conversation.

Installation

What a Proper Installation Involves

Flange Inspection

We check the floor flange for damage or the wrong height before setting a new toilet, since a bad flange causes leaks no matter how good the toilet is.

Wax Ring Seal

A properly seated wax ring is what actually prevents leaks and sewer gas smell at the base.

Level & Secure

An unlevel toilet stresses the wax seal over time and eventually leaks, so we confirm it's properly shimmed and bolted.

Test Flush

We run multiple test flushes before calling the job done, not just a visual check.

Repair or Replace?

When Repair Makes Sense vs. Replacement

Likely a Repair

  • Running, weak flush, or wobble
  • Tank and bowl are intact, no cracks
  • Unit is otherwise in good shape

Worth Discussing Replacement

  • Cracked tank or bowl
  • Repeated repairs on an aging unit
  • Upgrading to a modern low-flow model
